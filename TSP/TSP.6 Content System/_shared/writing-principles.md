# Writing principles

> **Draft 1, 2026-08-08.** Replaces `voice-profile.md` (personal-voice calibration), archived at `archive/voice-profile-personal-2026-08-08.md`.
>
> These are **universal**. They bind every persona in `personas/`, and they are not about any one author — they're the floor under all of them. A persona says *how* a piece is built. This file says what no piece may do, whoever is writing it.
>
> **Expected to evolve.** Add a principle when a real draft fails in a way nothing here catches. Remove one that never fires. Each entry records where it came from, so a future revision can tell an evidenced rule from an inherited one.

---

## 1. Don't invent

No fabricated quotes, metrics, sources, customers, examples, or events. No composite characters presented as one real person. Nothing "typical" or "representative" standing in for something that actually happened.

If a piece needs a detail the author didn't supply, the detail is missing — asking is the fix, not filling.

*Source: creative-nonfiction's two cardinal rules, "do not add and do not deceive"; the composite-character prohibition. Already in the skill's fabrication ban; restated here because it's the first principle, not a footnote.*

## 2. Don't supply substance

The author supplies what the piece claims. Craft supplies how it reads. A persona may shape structure, rhythm, and sequence; it may never originate a claim, a position, a doubt, or a stance.

**The structural corollary — this is the one that actually fires.** A form can demand content the author never provided. An argument shape needs an argument; a "what I learned" shape needs a lesson; a contrarian shape needs something to push against. When the form asks for material the author didn't give, the form is wrong for the material. Change the form. **Never manufacture the missing part to complete the shape.**

**The empty-beat variant.** Refusing to invent is not enough on its own. A form that demands a beat the material can't fill also produces sentences with the *shape* of content and none in it — a closing "next step" that names no step, a bridge asserting a connection that isn't there, a "what I learned" that restates the story. This passes the fabrication check, because nothing was made up, and it still fails: the author reads it and thinks *that's obvious, it says nothing*.

**A beat with no material is dropped, not filled.** Every persona's structure is a menu of what a piece can do, never a checklist it must complete.

*Source: the skill's direction-of-flow rule, extended. Evidenced 2026-08-08, twice in one piece: a draft built on an argument-shaped template invented a self-directed question, a root-cause diagnosis, and an unresolved doubt, none of which the author held — the original rule was worded around facts and ideas and did not catch a violation arriving through structure. Then, after that was fixed, the same piece closed on "The next tools are the ones I haven't reached yet," an empty beat written to satisfy the persona's closing move, which the author flagged as saying nothing. Same cause, one degree milder.*

## 3. Don't sound like generic AI

The full avoid-list and self-check live in `.claude/skills/content-post-writer/references/anti-ai-standards.md`. Not duplicated here.

The short version: no sterile transitions, no motivational-poster verbs, no "not just X, but Y" contrast frames, no em-dash habit, no decorative emoji, no "experts say" padding.

## 4. Cut everything that isn't working

Strip each sentence to its cleanest components. Kill qualifiers (*really, very, quite*), throat-clearing (*in order to, due to the fact that, it is important to note*), and any word doing no work.

Assume a first draft can lose a large fraction of its words without losing information or voice. Cutting is the default edit; adding is the exception that has to be argued for.

*Source: Zinsser, `On Writing Well` — simplicity as the essence, clutter as the disease.*

## 5. Short words, active voice, everyday language

Never a long word where a short one does. Never the passive where the active works. Never a jargon or foreign term where an ordinary English one says the same thing. Never a figure of speech you're used to seeing in print.

*Source: Orwell, "Politics and the English Language," rules 1–5.*

## 6. Break any of these rather than write something clumsy

Orwell's sixth rule, and it governs the other five here as much as it did there. A principle followed into an ugly sentence has failed. Rules are the floor, not the ceiling.

*Source: Orwell, rule 6.*

## 7. If you wouldn't say it, don't write it

Read it back as speech. A sentence you'd never say aloud to someone you respect is a sentence to rewrite.

This one doubles as a diagnostic: when a sentence sounds wrong in the ear, the thought underneath is usually still half-formed. Fix the thought, don't decorate the sentence.

*Source: Paul Graham, "Write Like You Talk."*

## 8. The reader is the point

Useful, honest, and about them. A piece that informs nobody, or that's only interesting to the person who wrote it, hasn't earned publication regardless of how well it's made.

*Source: Ann Handley, `Everybody Writes` — utility × inspiration × empathy, where a zero on any factor zeroes the whole thing.*

## 9. Be specific

Name the thing. Real numbers, real tools, real dates, real people. Abstraction is where writing goes to sound competent and say nothing.

Constrained by principle 1: specific means *actually specific*, not invented detail dressed as specificity.

*Source: Ogilvy on specificity over wit; Zinsser on concrete detail. Also the strongest single defence against principle 3.*

## 10. Don't tidy away a tension the author left standing

When the author says two things that pull against each other, and means both, leave them pulling. An editor's instinct is to resolve the contradiction; the honesty is usually the point, and resolving it silently changes what's being claimed.

Ask before resolving. Never resolve by default.

*Source: carried over from the archived personal voice profile, where it was the one observation that generalized beyond its author. Reclassified here as an honesty rule rather than a stylistic quirk.*

## How these interact with personas

Principles are **binding and silent** — applied every time, never negotiated with the author mid-draft.

Personas are **selected and visible** — proposed at Phase 1 with a reason, confirmed or overridden by the author.

When a persona's craft advice collides with a principle, the principle wins. A persona that can only work by breaking one is a persona with a bug, and the fix goes in the persona file.
