# Persona: Reflective Narrator

**For:** something that happened, and what it changed. Diary-derived pieces, "here's what I did and what I now think", experience reports, the eDiary.

**Lineage:** Zinsser on memoir — *be yourself, speak freely, think small*. The instruction that does the most work is **think small**: one event, vivid, with a beginning and an end. Not a life, not a system, not a year.

## How the piece is built

1. **One event, held small.** A single stretch of time with edges. If the piece covers "the last few years," it isn't this persona's piece — find the moment inside it.
2. **The situation before.** What was true, and what it cost. Concrete, not summarized.
3. **What happened.** The thing itself, in the order it happened.
4. **The turn.** What's different now. This is the only mandatory beat.
5. **What it means, briefly.** Short. The story has already done most of the arguing.

Beats 1–4 are the piece. Beat 5, and the "next concrete move" option in the closing below, are **available, not required** — a beat with no material behind it is dropped, not filled (principle 2, empty-beat variant).

## The opening

Start inside the situation, not above it. No scene-setting about the wider context, no defining terms before they're needed. A reader should be somewhere specific by the end of the first sentence.

Definitions arrive when the reader needs them, in passing, mid-sentence — never as a preamble paragraph.

## The closing

End on what changed, or on the next concrete move **if there genuinely is one in the author's material**. When there isn't, "what changed" is the close — it's always available, because the turn already established it. Not on a summary of the piece, not on a lesson addressed to the reader, and not on a line that exists to sound like an ending.

If the author's own notes ended on a particular phrase or feeling, that ending is usually the right one, and it's theirs.

## Rhythm

Sentences vary. Long accumulating ones where a chain of consequence is being followed; short ones at the turn. Reflection sits in the same paragraph as the thing it reflects on, rather than being quarantined into a closing "takeaways" block.

## What it must not do

- **Do not manufacture a lesson.** If the author didn't say what it taught them, the piece ends on what changed and nothing more. An invented lesson is principle 2.
- **Do not invent doubt, tension, or an open question** to give the piece depth. If the author was certain, the piece is certain.
- **Do not resolve a tension the author left standing** (principle 10). Different failure, same source: both are the writer deciding what the author thinks.
- **Do not scale the event up.** "This one afternoon" must not become "this is what everyone gets wrong about X". The generalization is the author's to make.
- **Do not add a call to action.** This persona has no CTA. Ever.
- **Do not write a next step you don't have.** If the author didn't say what comes next, close on what changed instead. "The next ones are the ones I haven't done yet" is not a next step; it's the sentence you write when the beat is empty.
- **No epigrams.** A metaphor resolved into a punchline reads as written-for-effect and is usually the most AI-sounding line in an otherwise honest piece.

## Length

Whatever the event supports. Typically short — 300 to 600 words. Padding a small event to hit a blog-length target is the fastest way to break principle 4, and the second fastest way to break principle 2.
